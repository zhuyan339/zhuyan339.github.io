Behavioral Science · Data · Real-World Intervention

My path has moved between psychology, behavioral data science, and direct behavioral intervention. Across these experiences, I keep returning to a simple but difficult problem: knowing what to do—or having a behavioral strategy available—does not always mean that it will be used when it matters.

I have encountered versions of this problem in different places: in longitudinal behavioral data, digital intervention engagement, and direct behavioral practice. These experiences have made me increasingly interested in the gap between behavioral capacity and action, and in how data can help us understand when and why that gap emerges.

This site documents how that question is developing through my research, practice, and ongoing work—and how I hope eventually to translate behavioral research into interventions, assessments, and tools that can be tested in real-world environments.

My Path

My research interests did not begin with a single theory or research question. They developed gradually as I moved between studying behavior, analyzing behavioral data, and working directly in intervention settings.

Foundation

Psychology

I began by studying psychology and became interested in how behavior develops, changes, and responds to social and environmental conditions. This gave me the theoretical foundation from which I later approached both data and intervention.

Graduate Training

Behavioral Data Science

During my master's training at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, I learned to approach behavior through data: longitudinal measurement, statistical modeling, computational workflows, and reproducible analysis. Working with real-world behavioral datasets made variation across people, time, and situations increasingly important to me.

Applied Experience

Behavioral Intervention in Practice

Direct behavioral intervention gave me a different way of thinking about behavior. A skill that appears established in one setting may not transfer reliably across people, routines, or environments. Seeing this variation in everyday practice made questions of implementation and generalization increasingly concrete to me.

Current Development

Connecting Data, Behavior, and Intervention

I am now trying to connect these parts of my background. Practice helps me notice behavioral problems that may be easy to miss in abstract models; data gives me a way to examine those problems systematically. I am especially interested in what happens between acquiring a behavioral capacity and actually using it in everyday life.

Projects & Questions

These projects represent different stages of my training. Some were collaborative research experiences; others are analyses I have continued to develop independently. I see them not only as projects I have completed or contributed to, but also as places where new behavioral questions began to emerge.

Independent Analysis · 2026–Present

From Repertoire to Implementation

Work in Progress

This project began with a question about protective behavioral strategies: does having a stronger repertoire of strategies translate into actually using those strategies during everyday drinking occasions?

I have been exploring this question through secondary analysis of intensive longitudinal daily-diary data in R, using person-mean decomposition, mixed-effects models, count and ordinal models, diagnostic checks, and sensitivity analyses.

What I am learning: This project has made the distinction between behavioral repertoire and behavioral implementation increasingly important to me. Knowing that a strategy exists in someone's repertoire tells us something—but not necessarily whether it will be used at the moment it matters.

Where this question is taking me: I am becoming interested in whether intensive longitudinal data can help identify the people, moments, and contexts in which the gap between behavioral capacity and implementation is most likely to emerge.

Graduate Research · 2024

Behavior Across Time

At the University of Wisconsin–Madison Addiction Research Center, I worked with more than 70,000 ecological momentary assessment observations from 336 participants in research on behavioral predictors of alcohol relapse.

My work included exploratory data analysis, data-quality validation, behavioral pattern exploration, and reproducible HTML reporting in R.

What changed in how I think: Working with repeated measurements made "the average person" feel increasingly incomplete as a way of thinking about behavior. The same person can behave differently across days and situations, and those fluctuations may contain information that a single baseline or cross-sectional measure cannot capture.

Graduate Data Science Capstone · 2023

Digital Intervention Engagement

For my graduate capstone with Healthy Minds Innovations, I worked with more than 5GB of application and survey data from a digital mindfulness intervention.

I used Python and process-mining methods to examine user engagement pathways and explored relationships between meditation engagement and well-being outcomes.

What changed in how I think: This project was one of the experiences that made me distinguish between making an intervention available and understanding how it is actually used. People can engage with the same intervention in very different ways, making engagement itself an important behavioral process to understand.

Collaborative Research · 2023–2024

PIVOT Lab

In the Preventing Interpersonal Violence and Overcoming Trauma Lab at UW–Madison, I supported research through data cleaning, variable construction, exploratory data analysis, statistical analysis, and visualization in R.

I also developed and refined funnel plots and other meta-analytic visualizations for a doctoral research project.

What stayed with me: This experience made me appreciate how much of research happens before a final model or result appears. Decisions about how behavior is represented, cleaned, summarized, and visualized can shape which patterns become visible and how they are interpreted.

Learning From Practice

Some of the questions I care about most did not begin with a dataset. They began with small discrepancies I noticed in everyday behavioral work.

A skill demonstrated in one setting may not appear as reliably in another. A strategy that works within one routine may become harder to use when the environment, people, prompts, or demands change. Behavior that looks stable during structured intervention can become much more variable in everyday contexts.

I do not treat these observations as research findings. Instead, they help me notice questions that can later be examined systematically. Practice has made me increasingly interested not only in whether an intervention or strategy works, but also in when behavior transfers, when implementation breaks down, and what changes in the surrounding context when that happens.

1. Notice

Real-world practice exposes variation that can be difficult to see in structured settings: the same skill, strategy, or intervention can look different across people, routines, and environments.

2. Ask & Measure

Those discrepancies can become researchable questions. Behavioral measurement and longitudinal data provide a way to examine when behavior changes and what individual or contextual factors accompany that change.

3. Return to Application

The goal is not for analysis to end with a model. What we learn can inform how interventions, assessments, or tools are designed, tested, and adapted for use in real-world settings.

Questions I Notice in Everyday Work

  • Why can a behavior look stable in one environment and fragile in another?
  • When does a learned skill become something a person can use independently across situations?
  • How do caregivers, practitioners, routines, and environmental demands shape whether an intervention is implemented as intended?
  • What would we need to measure in everyday life to know when additional behavioral support is most useful?

How I Work With Behavioral Data

My quantitative training gives me tools for turning behavioral questions into analyses. I am most interested in methods when they help clarify a behavioral problem—not as an endpoint by themselves.

Repeated Behavior

I am especially interested in data that capture behavior repeatedly across time and situations. Longitudinal and multilevel approaches allow me to distinguish relatively stable differences between people from changes occurring within the same person across occasions.

From Messy Data to Behavioral Variables

Much of my research experience has involved turning real-world behavioral data into analyzable representations: cleaning and transforming data, constructing variables and scales, exploring distributions and patterns, visualizing results, and checking whether analytic decisions change the conclusions.

Modeling as a Tool

I use R and Python for statistical and computational analysis, including mixed-effects, count, ordinal, and predictive approaches. My work has also included process mining, model diagnostics, sensitivity analysis, and reproducible research workflows.

Connecting Measurement to Practice

My applied behavioral training gives me another lens on measurement. Direct intervention has made me attentive to how behavior is defined, observed, prompted, reinforced, and generalized across settings—and to the difference between what can be measured conveniently and what may matter in everyday life.

What's Next

The question I am developing now sits at the intersection of behavioral science, measurement, and intervention.

Why do behavioral capacities or strategies that people possess fail to translate into implementation in real-world contexts—and how can behavioral data help determine when and how additional support or intervention is needed?

I am interested in studying when, for whom, and under what conditions implementation succeeds or breaks down. I want to better understand how individual, interpersonal, and environmental contexts shape the translation from behavioral capacity to action.

One direction I want to explore is whether repeated behavioral measurement can help identify moments when a person has the capacity or strategy needed for a behavior but is unlikely to implement it. That could create a more precise bridge between behavioral measurement and decisions about when additional support may be useful.

Over time, I hope to connect this research to the design and evaluation of interventions, assessments, and human-centered tools that can respond to behavior in real-world settings. I am interested in technology when it makes that translation possible, rather than as an endpoint in itself.

Doctoral training would allow me to develop these questions more rigorously: connecting behavioral theory with intensive measurement, testing when implementation succeeds or fails, and translating what is learned into interventions and tools that can themselves be evaluated in real-world settings.
