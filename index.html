Behavioral Science · Data · Practice

Irvine, California

I began in psychology, learned to study behavior through data, and later found myself working with behavior directly in everyday life.

Across those experiences, one question kept returning: why does knowing or learning what to do not always translate into doing it when it matters?

How I Got Here

Psychology

I started by wanting to understand behavior.

Psychology gave me a way to ask why people learn, respond, and change differently.

Data Science

Then I learned to measure it.

Graduate training taught me to look at behavior across people, time, and situations—and to turn messy behavioral data into questions I could test.

Practice

Then behavior became personal.

Working directly with children and families showed me something data alone could not: a skill learned in one moment does not automatically appear in the next person, place, or routine.

Now

I want to connect the two.

I want real-world experience to generate better research questions—and research to return as better ways of supporting behavior in everyday life.

Selected Work

Different projects have changed how I think about behavior in different ways.

Independent Research · 2026–Present

From Repertoire to Implementation

I am using daily behavioral data to ask whether having protective strategies available actually translates into using them during real-world drinking occasions.

It made me ask: When does behavioral capacity become action?

Addiction Research · 2024

Behavior Across Time

Working with intensive longitudinal alcohol-use data showed me how differently the same person can behave across days and situations.

It stayed with me: An average can hide the moments that matter.

Graduate Capstone · 2023

Digital Intervention Engagement

I studied how people actually engaged with a digital mindfulness intervention over time.

It made me notice: Making an intervention available does not mean people will use it.

Collaborative Research · 2023–2024

PIVOT Lab

Working with behavioral and survey data taught me how much careful preparation happens before a final result ever appears.

It taught me: How we represent behavior in data shapes what we are able to see.

Moments From Practice

Some of the questions I now bring to research began with small moments while working directly with children and families.

Progress did not always look like a dramatic change. Sometimes it was a new request, a response that needed one less prompt, or a skill appearing in a place where I had never seen it before.

Milestone 01

Finding a Voice

Watching a request move from heavy support toward more independent communication changed how I thought about what a small behavioral change can mean in everyday life.

Milestone 02

Beyond One Setting

A skill that appeared consistently in a structured setting could look very different when the people, routine, or environment changed.

Milestone 03

Small Changes Matter

Repeated work taught me to notice changes that are easy to overlook—and to ask what helped a new behavior become more independent and durable.

What I'm Asking Now

When people have the capacity to do something, what determines whether that capacity becomes action in everyday life?

I want to use behavioral data to understand when that translation succeeds or breaks down—and eventually use what we learn to build better interventions, assessments, and tools.
