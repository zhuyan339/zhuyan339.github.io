Behavioral Science · Data · Real-World Intervention

My path has moved between psychology, behavioral data science, and direct behavioral intervention. Across these experiences, I have become increasingly interested in a simple but difficult question: why does knowing what to do—or having a behavioral strategy available—not always translate into what people actually do in everyday life?

I am still developing this question. My experiences in research, data analysis, and applied practice have each shaped a different part of how I think about it. This site is a record of that development: the projects I have worked on, the questions that emerged from them, and the direction I hope to explore next.

I am especially interested in how behavioral research can eventually move beyond analysis into interventions, tools, and systems that can be tested and used in real-world environments.

My Path

My research interests did not begin with a single theory or research question. They developed gradually as I moved between studying behavior, analyzing behavioral data, and working directly in intervention settings.

Foundation

Psychology

I began by studying psychology and became interested in how behavior develops, changes, and responds to social and environmental conditions. This gave me the theoretical foundation from which I later approached both data and intervention.

Graduate Training

Behavioral Data Science

During my master's training at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, I learned to approach behavior through data: longitudinal measurement, statistical modeling, computational workflows, and reproducible analysis. Working with real-world behavioral datasets made variation across people, time, and situations increasingly important to me.

Applied Experience

Behavioral Intervention in Practice

Direct behavioral intervention gave me a different perspective. Skills and strategies that appear clear in structured programs do not automatically transfer across people, settings, caregivers, or everyday routines. These experiences made questions of implementation and generalization feel less abstract.

Current Development

Connecting Data, Behavior, and Intervention

I am now trying to connect these parts of my background. Rather than treating practice as research evidence, I use it as a source of questions—and use behavioral data and systematic research to examine those questions more carefully.

Projects & Questions

These projects represent different stages of my training. Some were collaborative research experiences; others are analyses I have continued to develop independently. I see them as part of an ongoing process of learning how to ask better behavioral questions.

Independent Analysis · 2026–Present

From Repertoire to Implementation

Work in Progress

This project began with a question about protective behavioral strategies: does having a stronger repertoire of strategies translate into actually using those strategies during everyday drinking occasions?

I have been exploring this question through secondary analysis of intensive longitudinal daily-diary data in R, using person-mean decomposition, mixed-effects models, count and ordinal models, diagnostic checks, and sensitivity analyses.

What I am learning: The analysis has pushed me to think beyond whether a strategy is known or available and toward the conditions under which it is actually implemented.

Question it led to: When does behavioral repertoire become real-world behavior, and what explains the gap when it does not?

Graduate Research · 2024

Behavior Across Time

At the University of Wisconsin–Madison Addiction Research Center, I worked with more than 70,000 ecological momentary assessment observations from 336 participants in research on behavioral predictors of alcohol relapse.

My work included exploratory data analysis, data-quality validation, behavioral pattern exploration, and reproducible HTML reporting in R.

What I learned: Behavioral data collected repeatedly in daily life can reveal variation that disappears when behavior is reduced to a single average or cross-sectional measure.

Graduate Data Science Capstone · 2023

Digital Intervention Engagement

For my graduate capstone with Healthy Minds Innovations, I worked with more than 5GB of application and survey data from a digital mindfulness intervention.

I used Python and process-mining methods to examine user engagement pathways and explored relationships between meditation engagement and well-being outcomes.

What I learned: The existence of an intervention does not guarantee meaningful engagement with it. Understanding how people actually use an intervention can be as important as the intervention itself.

Collaborative Research · 2023–2024

PIVOT Lab

In the Preventing Interpersonal Violence and Overcoming Trauma Lab at UW–Madison, I supported research through data cleaning, exploratory analysis, variable construction, visualization, and quantitative and qualitative work in R.

I also developed and refined funnel plots and other research visualizations for doctoral work.

What I learned: Collaborative research taught me how much careful data preparation, documentation, and interpretation sit behind the final results of a research project.

Learning From Practice

My applied behavioral work has shaped the kinds of questions I notice. Working across structured, home, and community settings has repeatedly reminded me that behavior change occurs within relationships, routines, environments, and everyday constraints.

I do not treat observations from practice as research findings. Instead, I see practice as one place where important research questions begin. It has made me especially curious about generalization, implementation, caregiver and practitioner behavior, and the conditions that help behavioral change persist outside structured intervention settings.

What's Next

The question I am developing now sits at the intersection of behavioral science, measurement, and intervention.

Why do behavioral capacities or strategies that people possess fail to translate into implementation in real-world contexts—and how can behavioral data help determine when and how additional support or intervention is needed?

I am interested in studying when, for whom, and under what conditions implementation succeeds or breaks down. I want to better understand how individual, interpersonal, and environmental contexts shape the translation from behavioral capacity to action.

Over time, I hope to connect this research to the design and evaluation of interventions, assessments, and human-centered tools that can respond to behavior in real-world settings.

I am particularly interested in doctoral training that would allow me to move repeatedly between research and application: using real-world problems to generate research questions, testing those questions systematically, translating what is learned into interventions or tools, and then learning from how those approaches perform outside the laboratory.

This direction is still developing. That is part of why I am pursuing doctoral training.
